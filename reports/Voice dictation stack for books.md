# Build a patient stenographer, not a typist

Build your own small tool rather than adopting an existing dictation app, and build it as a batch pipeline: record each session to disk, split it on pauses, transcribe locally, have an LLM produce a tightly constrained fair copy, and revise that copy as Markdown in git. Every Linux dictation app found is a "hold a hotkey, paste at the cursor" tool for short utterances; none keeps audio, a raw transcript and a clean copy linked the way a book needs. Your hardware is enough for the local route, but only on paper: the fit of Whisper large-v3-turbo (about 1.5 GB VRAM in int8) and Parakeet-TDT-0.6b-v3 (CPU only) is inferred from benchmarks on other machines, and nobody measured a GTX 1650. Published Spanish word error rates sit around 3 to 5% for every serious local model, but they ignore punctuation and no source tested Latin American accents, literary vocabulary or Spanish dialogue typography. Cloud transcription is a cheap second opinion (roughly $1 to $15 for the whole book), yet the only per-vendor Spanish numbers come from a vendor-run benchmark that was extracted automatically and needs rechecking. The LLM stage carries the real risk to your voice: research shows that "preserve the voice" prompts reduce drift but do not stop it, so the control has to be a mechanical word-level diff, not a prompt. The first job is therefore a bake-off on thirty minutes of your own dictated prose, because no published figure answers the question for your voice.

## Snitkina worked overnight, which argues for batch over streaming

The historical method was asynchronous. Dostoevsky made rough sketches alone, dictated from about noon to four with tea breaks, and Anna Snitkina transcribed her shorthand into a clean longhand copy at home in the evenings; the dictation ran 26 days, from 4 to 29 October 1866 Old Style ([Hermitage Fine Art](https://hermitagefineart.com/en/lots/2023-june-manuscripts/806/), [The Marginalian](https://www.themarginalian.org/2016/02/15/anna-dostoyevsky-reminiscences-marriage/)). The pressure came from the Stellovsky contract, which required a novel by 1 November 1866 or cost him the rights to his works for nine years ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gambler_(novel))). He initially dictated too fast and had to settle into a conversational pace ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anna_Dostoevskaya)). Two popular parts of the story are not confirmed by the sources in the notes: that he corrected her pages the next day before dictating further, and the anecdote about lodging the manuscript at a police station. Check both in Anna's *Reminiscences* or Joseph Frank before repeating them. Sources also differ by a day or two on the completion date, partly from Old Style versus New Style calendars.

Modern authors who dictate books work the same way. Kevin J. Anderson records drafts on a handheld recorder while hiking and has them transcribed ([KJA blog](https://kjablog.com/on-being-a-dictator/)). Monica Leonelle reports going from about 2,000 to "3500-4000+ words per hour" ([Leonelle's Substack](https://theworldneedsyourpassion.substack.com/p/introduction-to-dictation)); that figure is self-reported by an author selling a dictation book, so treat it as an upper bound. The consistent advice is that dictation is a learned skill and that drafting must be separated from editing ([Kindlepreneur](https://kindlepreneur.com/how-to-dictate/)). All of these accounts are from Anglophone authors; no Spanish-language account of dictating fiction was found.

The design consequence is that **live word-by-word display is optional and transcript quality is not**. The simplest robust design is utterance-level: Silero VAD detects the end of a phrase, the finished segment goes to a batch model, and text appears a second or two after you pause. This gives the model whole-sentence context, which helps punctuation, and avoids the flicker of Whisper streaming wrappers. Those wrappers exist (LocalAgreement reports about 3.3 s latency; WhisperLiveKit bundles it with the newer AlignAtt policy), but their benchmarks ran on an H100 and their latency on a 4 GB card is unknown ([arXiv 2307.14743](https://arxiv.org/html/2307.14743), [WhisperLiveKit](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/QuentinFuxa/WhisperLiveKit/main/README.md)).

If you later want to watch words form, one model fits the brief. **NVIDIA Nemotron 3.5 ASR Streaming 0.6b** (June 2026) is natively streaming, lists Spanish as "transcription-ready", emits punctuation, and reports Spanish FLEURS WER of 4.87% at 80 ms chunks and 4.11% at 1.12 s ([model card](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/nemotron-3.5-asr-streaming-0.6b)). Its card publishes no VRAM or CPU figures, so its fit on your machine is a size-class guess. Keep it as a later preview layer over the batch pipeline.

## Local engines reach 3 to 5% Spanish WER, on someone else's hardware

The models that top the English leaderboard in 2026 are large speech LLMs that need 16 GB or more of GPU memory. Voxtral Mini 4B Realtime, the best Spanish scorer found (2.71 to 3.31% FLEURS), requires "a single GPU with >= 16GB memory" ([model card](https://huggingface.co/mistralai/Voxtral-Mini-4B-Realtime-2602)). Your shortlist is the small models below.

| Engine | Spanish WER as reported | Fit on GTX 1650 / 12 threads (inferred) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whisper large-v3-turbo, faster-whisper | 6.91% Common Voice 17 (self-reported by a fine-tuner) | int8 about 1.5 GB VRAM, fp16 about 2.5 GB | MIT; free-text prompt for names; hallucination risk on silence |
| Whisper large-v3 | 4.89% MLS, 4.32% CoVoST2 | int8 about 2.9 GB, tight; fp16 about 4.5 GB does not fit | One community test showed int8 raising its WER |
| Parakeet-TDT-0.6b-v3 | 3.45% FLEURS, 4.39% MLS, 3.41% CoVoST | CPU alone; int8 ONNX about 670 MB | CC-BY-4.0; 18 to 30x real time on an i7-12700K |
| Canary-1b-v2 | 2.94% MLS, 3.81% CoVoST2 | Probably fits; needs 6 GB RAM to load; unverified | CC-BY-4.0; candidate for an offline second pass |
| Nemotron 3.5 ASR Streaming 0.6b | 4.11 to 4.87% FLEURS | Very likely; no published memory figures | True streaming; OpenMDW-1.1 licence |
| Moonshine Spanish Small Streaming | 4.9% (test set not identified) | Trivial | MIT; 123 M parameters |

Sources: [Whisper turbo fine-tune card](https://huggingface.co/adriszmar/whisper-large-v3-turbo-es), [NVIDIA paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.14128), [Parakeet v3 card](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v3), [Canary-1b-v2 card](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/canary-1b-v2), [Nemotron card](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/nemotron-3.5-asr-streaming-0.6b), [Moonshine docs](https://moonshine-voice.readthedocs.io/en/latest/models/available-models/), [faster-whisper issue #1030](https://github.com/SYSTRAN/faster-whisper/issues/1030), [Parakeet CPU benchmark](https://github.com/groxaxo/parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v3-fastapi-openai).

Read this table with four cautions. First, **no benchmark on a GTX 1650 or any 4 GB card was found** for any engine; the memory figures come from other GPUs, and the card also drives your desktop, so real headroom is below 4 GB. Second, the WER figures come from different test sets and normalisation schemes, so gaps of about one point are within noise. Third, all of them strip punctuation and casing before scoring, and no source measured Spanish punctuation, inverted marks or dialogue dashes. Fourth, the Parakeet CPU speed is self-reported by a community project on a faster CPU than yours, and the Canary and Whisper large-v3 MLS figures reached the notes through a search extract of the NVIDIA paper.

The practical differences are therefore not the headline WER. **Whisper** is the only family with an easy prompt for character names, but the prompt applies only to the first 30-second window ([HF forum](https://discuss.huggingface.co/t/adding-custom-vocabularies-on-whisper/29311)). VAD segmentation sidesteps that, because each utterance is its own first window. Whisper also has documented hallucination and repetition problems ([model card](https://huggingface.co/openai/whisper-large-v3-turbo)); Silero VAD plus `condition_on_previous_text=False` is the commonly recommended low-risk configuration ([faster-whisper](https://github.com/SYSTRAN/faster-whisper), [openai/whisper discussion](https://github.com/openai/whisper/discussions/679)). **Parakeet v3** avoids the CUDA stack entirely and, as a frame-synchronous transducer, should be less prone to inventing text during silence. That is an architectural inference, not a measurement. Its card documents no prompt mechanism, so names would be fixed downstream by the glossary.

On Debian 12, faster-whisper on the GPU needs the proprietary NVIDIA driver plus CUDA 12 and cuDNN 9 libraries, which pip can install ([faster-whisper](https://github.com/SYSTRAN/faster-whisper)). Licences are not an obstacle for a published book: Whisper and its runtimes are MIT, Parakeet and Canary are CC-BY-4.0. The OpenMDW-1.1 licence text for Nemotron was not reviewed.

## Cloud transcription costs under $15 a book, with weak Spanish evidence

Price does not separate the cloud vendors. A novel of 80,000 words is about 10 hours of continuous speech and perhaps 30 to 50 hours with retakes. At batch list prices that is about $3 to $5 on Soniox ($0.10/h), $6 to $10 on ElevenLabs Scribe v3 ($0.20/h), and $8 to $13.50 on OpenAI gpt-transcribe ($0.27/h) ([Soniox](https://soniox.com/pricing), [ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/pricing/api), [OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing)). Sign-up credits (Deepgram $200, Speechmatics $100) would cover the whole project ([Deepgram](https://deepgram.com/pricing), [Speechmatics](https://www.speechmatics.com/pricing)). Prices for Mistral, Google and Azure came from aggregators, not vendor pages, and should be re-verified.

Spanish accuracy is the weak point of the evidence. **No independent Spanish-only benchmark was found.** The only per-vendor Spanish table is AssemblyAI's own streaming benchmark, in which most modern models land at about 3 to 8% WER, with Speechmatics Enhanced (4.7 FLEURS / 2.5 Common Voice) and ElevenLabs Scribe v3 Live (6.8 / 4.0) among the strong group, and OpenAI's older gpt-4o-transcribe (12.1 / 8.9) and Deepgram Nova-3 (15.0 / 7.7) behind ([AssemblyAI benchmarks](https://www.assemblyai.com/benchmarks)). These numbers carry three caveats: the vendor ran the test, the values were extracted automatically from very long tables without manual verification of row alignment, and rankings swap between the two datasets. OpenAI's current gpt-transcribe was not in that table. No source tested Chilean or any other regional Latin American accent.

Cloud features suit an author better than local ones in two respects. Keyterm lists run to 1,000 terms on ElevenLabs and AssemblyAI, against Whisper's 224-token prompt, and Scribe accepts single files of up to 15 hours ([ElevenLabs docs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/overview/capabilities/speech-to-text), [AssemblyAI pricing](https://www.assemblyai.com/pricing)). Avoid "no verbatim" modes for fiction; they can silently delete deliberate repetition. No vendor was confirmed to support Spanish spoken punctuation commands.

Privacy defaults differ and several could not be verified. OpenAI's transcription endpoint is reported as no-training and no-retention by default, via a search summary of its data-controls page ([OpenAI](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/your-data)). Deepgram requires `mip_opt_out=true` per request ([Deepgram](https://developers.deepgram.com/docs/the-deepgram-model-improvement-partnership-program)). Speechmatics training is opt-in ([Speechmatics](https://www.speechmatics.com/pricing)). ElevenLabs reserves zero retention for select enterprise customers ([ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/docs/resources/zero-retention-mode)). Terms for Soniox, Mistral, Groq and Google were not confirmed from primary sources. For an unpublished manuscript, that uncertainty is a reason to keep the cloud as a comparison tool and local transcription as the default.

## The fair copy needs a mechanical guard, because prompts alone drift

The established pattern is a cascade: ASR, then a small LLM with a strict "you are a cleanup tool, not an assistant" prompt at low temperature, with fallback to the raw transcript on any failure ([AssemblyAI](https://www.assemblyai.com/blog/best-llms-dictation-cleanup), [bitvoice model card](https://huggingface.co/dhanr4j/bitvoice-dictation)). Published prompts converge on the same clauses: fix punctuation and capitalisation, remove fillers and false starts, change nothing else, treat the input as text and never answer it, output only the text.

That is not enough for literary prose. A 2026 study rewrote 300 personal narratives with three frontier LLMs and found function words, contractions and first-person pronouns falling while vocabulary diversity rose; **voice-preserving prompts shrank the shifts but did not reverse them** ([arXiv 2604.22142](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22142)). The research answer that fits this project is segment-wise correction with a cap on how much each segment may change, rejecting the LLM output and keeping the original when the cap is exceeded ([arXiv 2504.01519](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01519v3)). That study worked on Mandarin, and its 0.3 to 0.4 threshold does not transfer; your budget must be calibrated on your own sessions.

The concrete guard, inferred from that research and not found in any existing tool, works as follows. Normalise raw and cleaned text (lowercase, strip punctuation and accents) and align them word by word. Allow deletions only from a filler list and the abandoned half of a self-correction, allow substitutions only for spelling and glossary fixes, and never allow inserted content words. Anything else flags the segment or keeps the raw text. Punctuation, inverted marks, paragraph breaks and dialogue dashes vanish under normalisation, so correct formatting costs nothing. Repetition removal deserves the most conservative setting, since deliberate anaphora and a stammer look identical to a rule.

**Spanish dialogue with the raya (—) is the hardest part, and no product or published prompt handles it.** ASR output gives no reliable signal for where speech ends and the narrator's comment begins, so the LLM must infer it. The diff guard confirms no words changed but cannot confirm the layout is right; that stays a human check. Several of the RAE rules in the notes were recalled from memory because rae.es blocked the fetcher, so verify them against the [DPD entry](https://www.rae.es/dpd/raya) before encoding them in a prompt.

In-band voice commands are a poor fit for a novel, because characters say "punto" and "borra eso" in dialogue. Wispr Flow's documentation shows its LLM guessing from context and leaving ambiguous cases alone, with generative instructions behind a separate hotkey ([Wispr Flow docs](https://docs.wisprflow.ai/articles/5373093536-how-do-i-use-smart-formatting-backtrack), [Command Mode](https://docs.wisprflow.ai/articles/4816967992-how-to-use-command-mode)). Follow that layering: a separate key for instructions to the stenographer, at most a few reserved cues that count only as a whole utterance between pauses, and a second output field where the model notes doubts, as a stenographer would pencil a query in the margin.

Vocabulary belongs in a glossary file fed to both stages. Whisper's prompt is capped at 224 tokens ([OpenAI cookbook](https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/whisper_prompting_guide)), so the LLM prompt carries most of it, in a cached prefix of rules, glossary and a sample of your finished prose. Cache reads cost 0.1x base input or less on Anthropic models ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)).

Model cost does not discriminate either. The notes estimate one full cleanup pass over the novel at about $0.13 on Claude Haiku 5.5, $2.50 on Claude Sonnet 5.5 or Gemini 4 Argon, and $5 to $10 on flagships, from list prices and rough token assumptions ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing), [Gemini pricing](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/pricing)). These totals are the researcher's arithmetic, not vendor figures. Choose by measured fidelity on your own chapters. **No benchmark of any model on Spanish dictation cleanup was found.** The one benchmark available is vendor-run, English and synthetic; it shows top-tier models near-exact on short passages and small models degrading between 30 seconds and 2 minutes ([AssemblyAI](https://www.assemblyai.com/blog/best-llms-dictation-cleanup)). On that thin basis, a local 2 to 4B model in 4 GB VRAM is a privacy fallback for punctuation and filler removal, not a first choice for raya layout. One Mandarin study found a large reasoning model worse than no correction at all, which suggests disabling thinking modes ([arXiv 2504.01519](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.01519v3)).

Audio-native LLMs are not recommended as the transcriber. No head-to-head evidence shows them beating a cascade for verbatim dictation, and they merge hearing and editing into one step that cannot be diffed.

## GNOME 43 on Wayland makes text injection the worst place to start

Adopting an app means solving text injection on your exact desktop, where it is hardest. `wtype` does not work on GNOME ([scribe roadmap](https://perrette.github.io/scribe/roadmap-libei/)). The GlobalShortcuts portal needs GNOME 48 or later ([python-global-shortcut-portal](https://codeberg.org/marvin1099/python-global-shortcut-portal)), and Debian 12 ships GNOME 43. A local check found Debian's `ydotool` is the old 0.1.8 and `dotool` is not packaged. Whether libei injection works on GNOME 43 was not verified.

The open-source field is active but built for a different job. **Handy** (about 40k stars, MIT, Rust/Tauri, Whisper plus Parakeet V3, a `.deb`) is the reference implementation, and its README calls Wayland support "limited" ([Handy](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/cjpais/Handy/main/README.md)). voxtype's prebuilt binaries need glibc 2.38 and your system has 2.36 ([voxtype](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/peteonrails/voxtype/main/README.md)). Speech Note ships as a Flatpak with its own notepad buffer, and hyprwhspr has a long-form mode and lists Debian ([dsnote](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mkiol/dsnote/main/README.md), [hyprwhspr](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/goodroot/hyprwhspr/main/README.md)). Superwhisper launched a Linux app in November 2026 listing Debian, though whether that covers Debian 12 is unconfirmed ([AlternativeTo](https://alternativeto.net/news/2026/11/superwhisper-launches-on-linux-for-seamless-voice-input-across-major-distributions/)). Wispr Flow has no Linux build ([Wispr Flow](https://wisprflow.ai/downloads)), and Talon announced it is dropping Linux ([OSNews](https://www.osnews.com/story/145162/accessibility-input-tool-removes-x11-support-doesnt-want-to-support-wayland-users-caught-in-the-middle/)).

A tool that owns its own text output avoids the whole problem. It needs no uinput permissions and no compositor-specific code, and it provides what injection cannot: retained audio, re-transcription with a better model later, and a diff between raw transcript and fair copy. If you want system-wide dictation for email as well, install Handy for that and keep it separate.

Audio capture is solved. Your machine already has `pw-record`, so spawning it at 16 kHz mono and reading PCM from stdout is a zero-dependency path. Python's sounddevice through PortAudio is expected to work under PipeWire, but that was not confirmed for Debian 12 and needs a local test. Use Silero VAD (MIT) for segmentation ([silero-vad](https://github.com/snakers4/silero-vad)). Skip noise suppression: studies found denoising front ends degrade modern ASR ([arXiv 2512.17562](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.17562v1), [arXiv 2502.13982](https://export.arxiv.org/pdf/2502.13982)).

For the microphone, distance and room noise matter more than price. Whisper large-v2 scored about 16.9% WER on headset recordings of meetings against 36.4% from a distant microphone, a figure relayed by a secondary source and not checked against the original paper ([Wispr Flow blog](https://wisprflow.ai/dictation-blogs/speech-to-text-how-it-works)). A wired USB headset keeps the distance constant while you pace; avoid Bluetooth headset profiles. The notes found no trustworthy list of specific models, so none is recommended here.

## The recommended stack and the first two weeks

| Layer | Choice | Status of the evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Language | Python | Fastest iteration; same stack as hyprwhspr and Vocalinux |
| Capture | `pw-record` subprocess, 16 kHz mono, FLAC per session | Tools present on your machine |
| Microphone | Wired USB headset | Direction supported; no model tested |
| Segmentation | Silero VAD | Standard, MIT |
| Draft STT | faster-whisper large-v3-turbo int8 on GPU, names in a static prompt | Fit inferred, not measured on a GTX 1650 |
| Challenger STT | Parakeet-TDT-0.6b-v3 int8 ONNX on CPU | Speed from other hardware, self-reported |
| Second opinion | One or two cloud batch APIs on the same audio | Spanish ranking vendor-run and unverified |
| Fair copy | Mid or upper-tier cloud LLM, thinking off, low temperature, per segment | No Spanish cleanup benchmark exists |
| Fidelity control | Word-alignment diff with an edit whitelist | Design inferred from research; no existing tool |
| Manuscript | Markdown per scene, git, Pandoc | Well-trodden route |
| Later | Nemotron streaming preview, instruction hotkey, audio playback per paragraph | Nemotron fit unmeasured |

I put Whisper turbo first because it is the best-supported engine and the only one with a simple way to bias names; Parakeet may well win on your voice, and the evidence cannot say which. Keep the engine behind one interface so the swap costs nothing.

For storage, keep one directory per session holding the audio, the raw ASR output with word timestamps, the clean copy, and metadata recording model, prompt version and glossary hash. Keep audio out of plain git (git-annex, LFS or a synced folder). Commit the fair copy first as the stenographer's delivery and your revisions after it, so `git diff --word-diff` shows what you changed against what you dictated. Pandoc assembles chapter files into EPUB, PDF or DOCX ([Pandoc](https://pandoc.org/epub.html)). novelWriter is the closest ready-made plain-text alternative ([novelWriter](https://github.com/vkbo/novelWriter)); Scrivener has no Linux release. Enforce the real raya character (U+2014) in output; the Pandoc notes on Spanish typography were not verified against its manual.

Start with the measurement, not the application. Record thirty minutes of your own prose in Spanish through the headset, including dialogue and the book's names, and hand-correct a reference transcript. Run it through turbo on the GPU, Parakeet on the CPU, and two cloud APIs; this also settles the open questions about VRAM, speed and whether the CUDA libraries install cleanly on Debian 12. Compare word errors, then punctuation and names by eye. Next, write the capture, VAD and transcription loop that produces the session directory. Write the diff guard before the cleanup prompt, so every prompt experiment is scored mechanically from the first run. Then test three or four LLMs on the same raw sessions and count disallowed edits and raya errors. Only after a chapter has gone through the whole loop should you consider a revision interface, streaming preview or voice commands.

## Conclusion

The research changes the shape of the problem. Transcription, which looks like the hard part, is nearly a commodity: several engines land within a point or two of each other in Spanish and the whole book costs less than a paperback to transcribe. The parts that decide whether the result is still your prose are the ones nobody has measured: Spanish punctuation and dialogue layout, regional accent, and how far an LLM bends your sentences while tidying them. That is why the recommended build spends its effort on keeping the audio, keeping the raw transcript, and checking every machine edit against them.

It also means the project produces something beyond the book. Each session leaves a linked triple of audio, raw transcript and author-approved text, which is exactly the data needed to calibrate the edit budget, grow the glossary from your corrections, and eventually fine-tune a small local cleanup model on your own fair copies. Snitkina learned Dostoevsky's pace within days; the tool can only learn yours if it remembers what you said and what you kept.
